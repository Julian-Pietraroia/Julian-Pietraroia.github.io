Competition

Shooting & Catching Robots

Our Over Under robots. One launched triballs across the field, and my 15" robot caught them in an expanding net and delivered them to our goal.

For Over Under I was mechanical lead on the 15" robot. Our partner robot launched triballs across the field, and mine caught them in a net and pushed them into our goal.

The net starts inside the 15" x 15" x 15" size limit, and a lightweight linkage unfolds it to 4' tall and 2' wide at the start of the match. That gave us a big catching area without making the robot heavy or slow.

On the launcher, most of the work went into making every shot land in the same place: consistent feeding, compression and release.

It held up match after match, and the team won the VEX U Robotics Skills World Championship that year.

High Stakes

Ring Stacking & Shooting Robot

Our High Stakes robots could stack rings onto a goal with a conveyor and shoot them from range with a slingshot. Part of our Triple Crown at Worlds.

We built two robots that could both stack and shoot 7" rings, so we could score up close and from across the field in the same match.

The conveyor picks rings up and stacks them straight onto a goal the robot is holding, so it keeps scoring while it drives. The slingshot scores from a distance, which let us reach contested goals before the other alliance.

Fitting both onto one robot without making it heavy or fragile took most of the design time.

At the 2025 World Championship we won the Triple Crown: Tournament Champions, Skills Champions and the Excellence Award.

High Stakes

Climbing Robot

The High Stakes endgame is climbing the ladder in the last few seconds. The climber had to be light enough to carry all match and strong enough to lift the whole robot.

At the end of a High Stakes match, robots score by climbing the ladder in the middle of the field. Our climber had to lift the full weight of the robot, but it also rode around unused for the rest of the match.

That made it a weight trade-off. Every piece of structure I added made the climb safer and the robot slower, so I spent a lot of time working out where material was actually needed.

Autonomy

Line Following Robot

A fully autonomous robot that follows a line, avoids obstacles and draws a heat map of where it's been. It fuses encoder and IMU data to keep track of its position.

I designed and built this robot from scratch: a 3D printed chassis, hand-wired perfboard, motor drivers and gearmotors with encoders.

It follows the line with IR sensors and spots obstacles with a proximity sensor. When it has to leave the line to get around something, it combines wheel encoder odometry with IMU heading to keep track of where it is, then finds its way back.

It also logs everywhere it drives and turns that into a heat map.

Vision

Autonomous Ping Pong Turret

A turret that uses a camera to find a cup, works out the angle and power for the shot, and fires a ping pong ball into it from 1 to 5 m away.

The camera tracks each cup, and the turret calculates the launch angle and power for every shot.

To make shots repeatable I built a ratchet-driven puncher on a linear slide. One motor cocks it and fires it by reversing direction, so the whole firing mechanism runs on a single actuator.

Pan and tilt use DYNAMIXEL servos on a U2D2 bus with closed-loop control, so the turret stays locked on the target between shots.

Combat

1 lb Antweight Combat Robot

A 1 lb antweight with a brushless hub-motor spinner and a 4S brushless drivetrain, armoured with carbon-fibre nylon, TPU, titanium and AR-500 steel.

I designed this 1 lb antweight around a brushless hub-motor spinner and a 4S brushless drivetrain, and sized the motors and gearing for grip on a wood floor and fast weapon spin-up.

The chassis and armour are modelled in Fusion 360. I picked carbon-fibre nylon, TPU, titanium and AR-500 steel for the parts that take hits.

Right now I'm testing high-speed ducted fans that suck the robot down onto the floor, adjusting thrust and ground clearance to get more traction and pushing power.

Manufacturing

RC Car, Designed for 20,000 Units

A course project where we designed an RC car and costed every part for a 20,000-unit production run. I owned the roll cage, its injection mould and the aeroshell.

Our six-person team designed an RC car with a glass-fibre chassis, a 3D printed roll cage, cast aluminium rims and a thermoformed ABS shell, then worked out how to make 20,000 of them.

Costs per part: sand-cast A356 rims at $2.50 each, an injection-moulded ABS roll cage at $3.20 on a 9:50 cycle, a vacuum-thermoformed shell at $5.65 and a printed glass-fibre chassis extension at $4.42.

I was responsible for the roll cage, its injection mould, and the aeroshell design analysis and material selection. The drawings in this card are mine: the roll cage base and the halo mould, with ejector pins and a section view.

Tipping Point

Goal Lifting Robot

5th of 20,000+ teams in Tipping Point. A 4-bar grabs mobile goals, a conveyor loads 22 rings a match, and it climbs the platform at the end.

Built for the Tipping Point season, where we placed 5th out of more than 20,000 teams.

A 4-bar grabs mobile goals quickly and holds on to them when other robots push back. I designed the ring intake and conveyor in Onshape so it could load rings onto a goal while driving, 22 rings per match. In the endgame the robot climbs onto the platform and balances it.

We were a 3-person team, and I also helped write autonomous routines that changed depending on what our opponents did.

Tower Takeover

Block Stacking Robot

BC Provincial Champion. A roller intake and tilting tray that sets down a stack of nine 7" cubes in one move.

My first championship robot, which won the BC Provincial Championship in Tower Takeover.

Rollers pull 7" cubes into a tray, and the tray tilts up to set a stack of nine down in one motion. I modelled it in Fusion and went through three versions, each one stacking faster and more reliably, until it scored around 27 cubes a match.

It could also place cubes in towers while still holding more.
